# Nobody Warned Me About This Part of Building a Product...

CB Insights analysed 431 venture-backed companies that shut down since 2023. Running out of money appeared in 70% of the post-mortems. Poor product-market fit appeared in 43%.

At first, that sounds like a funding problem. Often, it was the final symptom of something that had gone wrong much earlier. The company built something people did not need badly enough, priced it badly, hired too quickly, or discovered too late that the product was harder to operate than it was to demo. [Read the CB Insights report](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/?6b6e84fd_page=0).

What nobody warned me about was how little the original idea matters once the work begins.

The idea gets you excited. The prototype makes it feel real. Then customers, costs, regulations, technical limits, and other people get involved. That is when the product stops belonging to your imagination.

That is also when building gets difficult.

## The idea was never the hard part

Product stories usually begin with a spark. Someone notices a problem, sketches a solution, stays up late building the first version, and eventually launches.

It is a clean story. It is also missing most of the work.

The first version gives you something to point at. It does not tell you whether anyone cares, whether the product works outside your controlled setup, or whether the business can afford to keep it alive.

After the excitement wears off, you have to answer less glamorous questions. Will anybody pay for this? Can they understand it without you standing beside them? What breaks when ten people use it at once? What happens when a supplier is late, an API changes, or a customer asks for a refund?

Those questions shape the product more than the original sketch ever will.

## Compliments are dangerously cheap

Early feedback feels good because most people are polite.

You show someone the idea. They call it interesting. They tell you they would use it. Then they suggest five features and disappear.

That is not validation.

A compliment costs nothing. Commitment costs something. A useful signal is a person paying, booking time to test the product, moving their data, introducing you to a decision-maker, or accepting the inconvenience of an unfinished version.

This is why feature requests can fool you. People are good at describing what sounds useful. Their behaviour tells you whether the problem matters enough to change what they already do.

The early work around products such as Linear and Notion is a useful reminder. The question is not whether users can imagine more features. It is whether they will make room for the product while it is still incomplete.

If nobody will risk time, money, or reputation on the idea, you may have an interesting concept. You do not have a product yet.

## Your prototype is probably lying to you

A polished prototype creates confidence very quickly.

The screens look right. The happy path works. Everyone in the room understands what is supposed to happen. It can feel like most of the product already exists.

Then a real user arrives with bad data, an older device, a slow connection, or a workflow you never considered.

The demo falls apart.

Hardware teams run into the same problem when a device that worked on stage struggles with heat, battery life, manufacturing differences, or daily wear. Software only hides the problem better. A clean interface can sit on top of slow queries, fragile integrations, confusing permissions, and expensive infrastructure.

The prototype is useful when it helps you find those problems early. Try bad inputs. Interrupt the process halfway through. Test it somewhere that does not resemble the room where it was built. Give it to someone who has not heard your explanation.

Do not protect the prototype. Try to break it.

## The boring work becomes the product

Operations sound like something that happens after the product is finished. That separation does not survive contact with customers.

For a physical product, component shortages, factory variation, packaging damage, delivery delays, and returns change what the team can promise. For software, API limits, unreliable providers, data rules, and hosting costs do the same thing.

Customers experience all of it as the product.

They do not care that the delay came from a supplier or that a third-party service failed. They care that the thing they paid for did not work.

This is where many attractive ideas become difficult businesses. The feature may be possible, but too expensive to support. The experience may work for twenty customers and collapse at two hundred. A promise made during sales can become a weekly emergency for the team.

Stripe's infrastructure is a useful example of the discipline behind a product that has to work when failure is expensive. The interface may look simple, but the operation underneath it cannot be casual.

You are not building the screen. You are building everything required to keep the promise on that screen.

## Legal problems do not wait for launch

Privacy, accessibility, intellectual property, platform rules, and industry regulations can change a product before it reaches the public.

This catches teams off guard because legal work feels like a final review. By then, the expensive decisions have already been made.

Consumer AI products ran into this publicly between 2024 and 2026. Questions about training data, responsibility for outputs, and regional rules forced some teams to redesign products quickly.

The lesson is not to let lawyers run the roadmap. It is to ask the uncomfortable questions while the product is still cheap to change.

Where did the data come from? What are you promising? Which users need access? What happens when somebody asks you to delete their information? Can the product legally operate in every market you are targeting?

Ignoring those questions does not make the company move faster. It moves the delay to a more expensive point.

## Your first hires will rewrite the product

The first ten hires do more than increase capacity. They change how decisions get made and what the company starts rewarding.

One person pushes for speed. Another wants polish. Someone follows customer requests closely. Someone else wants to protect the original idea. None of those instincts is automatically wrong, but together they can pull the product in different directions.

This part is messy. Ownership becomes unclear. Decisions take longer. People get tired. The product starts collecting compromises that made sense individually but look incoherent together.

Founders have to decide what can change and what must remain protected. Too much control stops the team from learning. Too little direction turns the roadmap into a pile of requests.

Hiring is product work because the people you hire will make product decisions when you are not in the room.

## Pricing exposes what you do not understand

Pricing is often postponed until the product feels ready. That is a mistake.

The moment you ask someone to pay, vague thinking becomes obvious. Who is the product for? Which problem is expensive enough to solve? What result is the customer buying? Can the company serve that customer without losing money?

A free tier can fill the product with users who never reach its main value. A cheap plan can attract customers who require more support than the price covers. A bundle can hide the one feature people wanted in the first place.

These are product decisions because they change who arrives and what those people expect.

Testing price can teach a team more than another roadmap meeting. People become more honest when money enters the conversation. So does the business.

## Launch is when the real product shows up

Launch feels like an ending because so much effort goes into reaching it. In practice, it is the first time the product meets enough people to prove how much the team misunderstood.

Support requests reveal confusing steps. Strange use cases expose needs nobody planned for. Workarounds show what users wish the product did. Silent churn is harder because the customer leaves without explaining which assumption failed.

The first release is evidence. It is not a verdict.

Teams that improve after launch pay attention to what people do, not only what they say. They speak to customers directly. They watch where users get stuck. They remove features that looked impressive in a demo but created friction in daily use.

The uncomfortable part is accepting that launch does not prove you were right. It gives reality a chance to correct you.

## Nobody puts this in the origin story

By the time a product succeeds, the story has usually been cleaned up.

The false starts disappear. The bad pricing decisions become lessons. The hiring mistakes, support problems, legal scares, and operational failures get compressed into a sentence about persistence.

That version is easier to tell, but it makes building look more mysterious than it is.

Products survive because teams keep paying attention after the exciting part is over. They ask for real commitment instead of compliments. They break prototypes before customers do. They treat operations, pricing, legal work, and hiring as part of the product. Then they keep listening after launch.

The idea matters. It gets the work started.

Everything that happens next decides whether the idea deserves to survive.
