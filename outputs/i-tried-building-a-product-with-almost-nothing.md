# I Tried Building a Product With Almost Nothing

I kept thinking about a line from James Clear: "You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems." [He wrote it in *Atomic Habits*.](https://jamesclear.com/quotes/from-atomic-habits-2)

At the time, I thought the line was about discipline. These days I hear something else in it. A product does not get built because you had one big burst of motivation. It gets built because you keep returning to the work after the excitement has gone.

I tried to build a product with almost nothing. I had a laptop, internet access, a half-formed idea, and the nerve to believe I could figure out the rest as I went.

That last part was doing a lot of work.

## Before it had a name

In early 2023, I was already working in a way people would later call "vibe coding." I did not have a name for it then. I had a problem, a blank screen, and a habit of asking a machine to help me make the next thing.

Claude became available more broadly in March 2023, and Mistral 7B followed in September. [Anthropic announced Claude on March 14, 2023](https://www.anthropic.com/news/introducing-claude?os=0), while [Mistral announced Mistral 7B on September 27, 2023](https://mistral.ai/en/news/announcing-mistral-7b). I remember the tools changing quickly enough that my process changed with them.

I would switch between Mistral and Claude, ask one to explain what the other had written, copy a block of code, paste it into Replit, and wait to see what broke.

Sometimes the answer worked immediately. More often, it worked just well enough to create a new problem.

I became a translator between confident machine-written code and the error message sitting in front of me. I did not understand every line. I understood that the red text meant I had another question to ask.

That was the system. Ask. Paste. Run. Read the error. Ask again.

It was not elegant, but it moved the product forward.

## The bill was part of the feedback

There is a particular kind of optimism that comes from building with cheap tools. You feel like you have found a shortcut around the old barriers. You do not need a big team. You do not need a perfect technical plan. You just need to keep going.

Then Replit sends you a bill.

Mine felt silly because the product was still mostly an experiment. I was paying to keep a thing alive while I was still trying to work out what the thing was. Every charge made the same point in a different way: even a small product has an operating cost.

The bill was annoying, but it was useful. It made the work less imaginary. A product is not only the screen you can show somebody. It is the hosting, the broken flow, the account, the database, the support question, and every other small responsibility attached to the promise.

I had wanted the fun part, which was making the idea visible. The bill introduced me to the part where the idea has to survive contact with reality.

## Pasting code is not the same as building

AI made it possible for me to move faster than my technical knowledge. That was the gift. It was also the trap.

I could paste code before I understood it. I could ask for a fix before I knew what had failed. I could get a page on screen and mistake that moment for progress.

The product looked closer to finished than it really was.

Every time I copied a solution, I inherited decisions I had not made. How should the data be stored? What happens when the user leaves halfway through? What happens when the input is wrong? Who can see the result? What happens when the free service stops being free?

The machine could help me answer those questions, but it could not decide which questions mattered to the person using the product. That part was still mine.

Coding was difficult because I was learning the language and building the thing at the same time. But the hardest part was not technical. The hardest part was knowing what deserved to exist in the first place.

## Then I submitted it to Product Hunt

Eventually, I submitted the product to Product Hunt.

That sentence sounds tidy now. It was not tidy then.

Submitting meant taking the thing out of my private loop of prompts, errors, fixes, and small victories. It meant putting a name and a description on it. It meant admitting that I had made something, even if I was still unsure whether it was any good.

Product Hunt's own launch guide describes the platform as a place where makers share products with early adopters and people looking for new tools. [The launch guide also frames a launch as a way to get distribution and feedback](https://www.producthunt.com/launch). That was exactly what I needed, although I did not fully understand it at the time.

I shared the launch. I watched the page. I wondered whether anybody would care.

The strange thing about putting a product online is that the fear gets louder right before the work becomes useful. While the product is private, every flaw can be explained away. Once it is public, the market gets a vote.

That vote may be quiet. It may arrive as a question, a click, a sign-up, a message, or no response at all. Silence is still information. It tells you that the story did not travel far enough, the problem was not urgent enough, or you did not reach the right person.

## The first 100 customers are a different kind of code

I used to think the hard part was coding the product.

That is true when you are starting from almost nothing. There are a lot of new words. There are errors you cannot yet read. There are moments when one missing bracket can make you question your entire life.

But getting the first 100 customers is harder in a different way.

Code gives you something to push against. A compiler complains. A browser shows you the wrong thing. An AI tool gives you another answer. Customers are less predictable. They do not owe you a clear bug report. They can like the idea and still not use it. They can say they want it and then forget to come back.

You have to find them, explain the product, listen without trying to win the conversation, notice what they do instead of what they say, and keep changing the thing without losing its purpose.

That work has no satisfying loading bar.

It is also where the product becomes real.

The first 100 customers are not just a growth target. They are 100 chances to discover whether your product belongs in somebody's routine. They show you which promise is clear, which feature is noise, and what people are willing to change in order to use what you made.

## What I would do differently

I would still use the tools. I would still paste code. I would still ask a model to explain a problem at two in the morning. That part was useful, and for someone with limited resources, it can be the difference between an idea staying in a notebook and becoming something people can try.

But I would talk to potential users earlier. I would write down the one problem the product was meant to solve before adding another feature. I would treat every cost as product feedback. I would launch with less certainty and pay closer attention to the people who took the trouble to respond.

I would also remember that getting a product to run is only one kind of progress.

The product can be live and still be waiting for its first real user. It can have a clean interface and no reason for anyone to return. It can be technically impressive and commercially invisible.

That is the part I understand better now. Coding is hard when you are learning it as you go. Finding the first 100 customers is hard because the work leaves your control.

I tried building a product with almost nothing. The laptop helped. The AI tools helped. Even the ridiculous Replit bill taught me something.

The thing that mattered most was putting the product in front of people before I felt ready, then staying close enough to reality to hear what happened next.
